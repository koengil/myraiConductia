# 2. Business Summary

**Conductia turns investment banking from a people-constrained services model into a software-leveraged execution platform that makes senior bankers more scalable, deal teams faster, and firms safer and more profitable.**

## Opportunity and solution

Investment banking is one of the world’s highest-value services markets, but it remains constrained by senior banker time. Conductia productizes the repeatable execution layer around senior judgment: drafting, coordination, human/AI task routing, diligence, obligation tracking, knowledge capture, review, compliance, and closing management. The result is greater revenue capacity from the same senior bench, lower execution cost and higher profits, higher quality, faster turnaround, and reduced legal, regulatory, and deal risk.

## The wedge: investment banking

Workflows are repetitive but judgment-intensive, willingness to pay is high, and founder-market fit is rare. Storm Duncan brings decades of technology M&A experience, elite customer access, regulatory fluency, and firsthand knowledge of how high-stakes advisory work actually runs. Jess Mah adds the company-builder lens: financial-workflow software experience, venture-capital credibility, scaling expertise, and the ability to turn expert-services workflows into software platforms.

## The expansion

The same architecture can become the AI operating layer for the broader professional-services market, where expert time is scarce, institutional knowledge is fragmented, and workflow risk is high.

# 3. MVP, Timeline, and Milestones

## 3.1 What v1 is (and is not)

v1 proves two things on one live deal: the operating spine (capture, plan, route, retrieve) and the compliance floor (permissioning, pre-send QC, recordkeeping), with a focused set of applications and standing panels that execute and check real deal work. It is not the full platform.

**The core journeys that must work end to end:**

1.  Create a deal: stand up a deal in minutes with code name, owner, team, and governance settings.

2.  Ingest deal artifacts: email, calendar, files, CRM, and VDR activity, normalized and permission-tagged.

3.  Generate and maintain the task plan: owner, execution mode, reviewer, approver, due date, dependencies, risk tier.

4.  Capture communications: emails, calls, and meetings summarized and preserved under code name.

5.  Daily digest and printable to-do lists: each banker gets a personalized morning email with a deep link into the Command Center, plus a one-click view of their daily, weekly, and monthly to-do lists, all printable, where they can see, execute, and update their own deliverables.

6.  Shared deal timeline and deal tracker: the whole internal team sees live status; buyer-facing views are permissioned so buyers cannot see other buyers’ status.

7.  Staffing view for senior bankers: a table by deal and by employee, with a deal-intensity ranking, so leadership can see who is loaded and how hard at any time.

8.  Pre-send QC: check every external item (recipient, attachment, code name, figures, claims, metadata, approval).

9.  Retrieve and ask: answer where the deal stands and any permissioned question, with citations.

10. Produce the weekly update: draft the client and internal update from the live record.

## 4.2 The selection test for first-release applications

The principle for what makes the first release is strict: an application ships at launch only if it is both highly valuable and can be executed flawlessly. Flawless execution is the gate, because trust is the whole product in a regulated setting. The test has two parts:

**Ships at launch:** work that is bounded, sourced, and human-gated. The task has a defined scope and a checkable answer (mechanical analysis like a DCF on management’s own projections, comps assembled from sourced data), or it drafts an artifact a human then approves and reworks (buyer list with an editable email, outreach, FAQ). A human signs off on anything that leaves the firm.

**Waits:** open-ended generative authoring of complex, judgment-heavy deliverables where being wrong is likely and costly (building the financial model itself, authoring the CIM or the management presentation, advocating a valuation to a counterparty, a negotiation copilot).

## 4.6 Applications must comply with the Command Center

Every application, panel, and skill runs inside the Command Center and inherits its rules. None of these is optional, and this is what lets a bank put AI on a live deal at all.

- Model router: every model call goes through the Command Center’s router (right model for the task by sensitivity, cost, latency, and quality), never a direct, unrouted call.

- Time and token tracking: every application’s human time and AI consumption is tracked by deal, person or agent, and task, feeding the cost and ROI metrics.

- Permissioning and information barriers: applications see only what the user or agent is cleared to see; code names and the access-logged key apply throughout.

- Regulatory and AI-governance rules: prompt and output logging with version tracking, agent runtime limits (step limits, loop detection, budgets, tool permissions, kill switch), and human approval gates on anything irreversible, regulated, or external.

- Recordkeeping: every action, source, output, and approval is preserved in a form compatible with the firm’s retention architecture.

# 9. Pricing Strategy

Goal: decide how Conductia charges. The constraint is set: no seat-based pricing. The opportunity is that the entire incumbent category (Rogo, Hebbia, AlphaSense) prices per-seat on seven-figure enterprise contracts, which the market openly criticizes as a poor fit for the boutique and mid-market firms that are our wedge. Rejecting seats is both true to our thesis (we price against labor value, not logins or tokens) and a wedge against how every competitor sells.

## 9.1 The recommendation: a platform fee anchored to deal capacity, plus a deal-linked component

A two-layer hybrid gives predictable, fundable revenue and the closed-deal linkage you want, without the problems of a pure success fee.

| **Layer** | **Share** | **What it is and why** |
|----|:--:|----|
| **Platform access fee, tiered by deal capacity** | ~70–80% | An annual subscription priced by the firm’s deal throughput (active mandates run on Conductia per year), e.g. tiers of up to 10 / up to 25 / unlimited live deals. Scales with the size and activity of the firm without counting heads, is predictable and recognizable as SaaS revenue, and lands in the mid-market white space incumbents price out of. Bankers intuitively understand a per-active-deal unit because it mirrors how they think about their own business. |
| **Deal-linked value component** | ~20–30% | The closed-deal linkage, structured to avoid success-fee problems. Two safer options: (a) a fixed deal-activation fee when a mandate is stood up (ties revenue to deal activity, not outcome), or (b) a flat, capped success-milestone fee on close (a flat amount per closed transaction, never a percentage of the bank’s fee). Open with (a); offer (b) to firms that want to share risk. |

## 9.2 Why not the obvious alternatives

**Pure success fee (a percentage of the bank’s deal fee).** Rejected as the primary model. Revenue is lumpy and long-dated (deals close in 6 to 12 months, many die), attribution is contestable (the bank argues the outcome was its relationships), and, most seriously, taking a contingent slice of a securities-transaction fee can look like sharing transaction-based compensation, which draws FINRA/SEC scrutiny for an unregistered vendor. We want deal linkage, not deal participation.

**Pure usage / tokens.** Rejected by our own blueprint. It caps value at cost, makes spend unpredictable for the CFO, and turns every renewal into a metering argument.

**Per-seat.** Off the table per the founders, and the incumbent model we position against.

## 9.3 Worked example (illustrative, to react to)

Illustrative tiers at three firm sizes, combining the capacity fee and a fixed per-deal activation fee. FLAG: these are placeholder numbers to anchor discussion, not researched price points. Validate with design partners.

| **Firm profile** | **Active deals/yr** | **Platform tier (illustrative)** | **Deal-linked (illustrative)** |
|----|:--:|:--:|----|
| **Small boutique** | up to 10 | \$150K–\$250K/yr | \+ \$10K activation per deal |
| **Mid-market bank** | up to 25 | \$400K–\$750K/yr | \+ \$10K activation per deal |
| **Larger advisory** | unlimited | \$1M+/yr | \+ activation or flat per-close |

**Reading the example:**

- Revenue scales with firm activity and value, not headcount, and stays predictable enough to recognize as SaaS and to underwrite a venture case.

- The deal-linked component creates alignment (we participate in deal activity) while the flat/capped structure avoids looking like a contingent commission.

- The small-boutique tier deliberately undercuts the incumbents’ seven-figure minimums, opening the wedge segment.

## 9.4 Principles to hold

- Price against value, not cost: never let it drift into a token pass-through.

- Land design partners on a discounted version of this exact structure, so the pilot tests the real model, not a throwaway.

- Keep it firm-configurable, the way everything else in Conductia is: a 6-person boutique and a 60-person mid-market bank will want different tier shapes.

- Tie the story to Net Labor Value Captured: in the pilot, show the labor value absorbed per deal and anchor the tier and per-deal fee to a fraction of it.

## 10.2 Structure: priced vs. SAFE, and QSBS

You said capital is the priority and QSBS a secondary optimization. The key interaction to understand:

- QSBS (qualified small business stock) eligibility runs from the original issuance of stock. A priced round issues stock now, starting the holding-period clock for the company’s shares issued in that round. A SAFE is not stock until it converts, so a SAFE can delay when the clock starts for those dollars.

- Recent 2025 federal changes (OBBBA) introduced tiered QSBS gain exclusion at 3, 4, and 5-year holds and raised the per-issuer cap and the gross-assets ceiling. This makes starting the clock earlier more valuable than under the old all-or-nothing five-year rule.

- Implication: a priced first tranche serves both priorities reasonably well, capital now and the QSBS clock started now. SAFEs are still useful for speed and for later tranches.
