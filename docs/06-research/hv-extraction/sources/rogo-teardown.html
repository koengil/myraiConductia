Executive summary

Rogo is a well-built tool for the wrong seat. Ten months of shipping automates the analyst who builds the model, not the senior banker who originates, judges, and runs the deal. It is reactive by design, it hides the three things it should be proudest of, and the differentiated future it has sketched exists only as mock prototypes with no backend.

The field evidence matches the code. Ignatious ran Rogo for three weeks, judged it weak, and reverted to their existing PowerPoint tool. The read on Rogo's real value to that bank was liability insurance, AI credibility, and junior-banker retention, not output quality. That is a narrow value proposition, and it is the room we are building into.

Rogo's genuine strengths, its licensed data, its security, its model router, are largely hidden from the person paying for it. For a platform aimed at the senior banker, that is exactly the ground to take.

This teardown reverse-engineers Rogo from a full capture of its web app, including all 264 client-side JavaScript bundles, so the findings rest on shipped code and feature flags rather than marketing, cross-checked against hands-on testing of the live product. It reconstructs Rogo's strategy, separates what Rogo does well from where it is exposed, and translates each finding into a build decision for the platform we are building for senior investment bankers.

The company behind the product

The reality check before the teardown: who Rogo is, how much they have raised, how they are distributed, what they claim, and the bear case already on the public record.

Before the teardown, the reality check. Rogo is not a scrappy target. It is the best-funded and best-distributed company in this category, and any honest read of where it is exposed has to start from that.

The takeaway is not that Rogo is unbeatable. It is that beating it is a positioning and execution problem against a well-capitalized category leader with marquee logos and a data-partnership moat, not a matter of pointing out that their app has no 2FA. The rest of this teardown is about where a leader this size is genuinely exposed.

What Rogo says it is

Rogo's own top line is "AI for the most ambitious firms in finance" and "the trusted AI partner to the world's leading financial institutions." That is a trust-and-enterprise frame, not a task-tool frame, and it is aimed at the top of the market. Its investors push it further: Kleiner Perkins frames the bet as the "operating system for investment banking," Bloomberg-in-the-1980s style, so deeply embedded that ripping it out becomes uneconomic.

The most important shift is in the verbs. Over the last year Rogo has moved its story from copilot to autopilot. The public claim now is that Felix "executes complex, multi-step financial processes autonomously, from deal screening and CIM generation to buyer outreach and data room diligence," that you "email Felix like a colleague," and that it works asynchronously and writes finished, cited work back into Excel, PowerPoint, and Word in the firm's own templates. Hold that claim next to the field reality below; the gap between the two is the most useful thing in this section.

The bear case, from people who are not us

The sharpest critiques of Rogo are already on the public record, and they are worth repeating because they are not our framing.

  • It does not own a model. Rogo does not pretrain a proprietary foundation model. Felix is a finance-specific agent harness plus fine-tuned third-party models, routed by a "Model Broker." Independent analysts (Sacra) call the dependence on OpenAI and Google a core structural vulnerability on pricing, access, and the risk that the labs or the banks build something better. AlphaSense sharpens it to an accuracy attack, calling Rogo "completely reliant on OpenAI's models, which are infamous for their propensity to hallucinate."
  • Its own data partners can bundle against it. Sacra notes that Bloomberg, FactSet, and S&P Capital IQ have entrenched bank relationships and could bundle competing AI at marginal cost to defend their seat licenses. Those are the same vendors whose data is Rogo's moat, which is a dependency and a threat at once.
  • The biggest banks build instead of buy. JPMorgan, Goldman, and Citi are building in-house LLM stacks, which caps the top of Rogo's market and points its real demand at middle-market and boutique firms.
  • It strains on large, unstructured document sets. Hebbia argues Rogo cannot reliably scale analysis over thousands of documents (a full data room, a firm's deal history) because of context-window limits, and that its investment-banking roots make it template-rigid and weak on bespoke diligence.
  • Citations are response-level, not sentence-level. Competitors frame Rogo's citations as auditable at the answer level but not traced to the sentence or the computation, which matters for a governance-first buyer.
  • The whole cohort is unproven. Forbes, surveying the AI-research field (Rogo, Hebbia, AlphaSense, and others), concludes plainly that "it's hard to tell which of these businesses will live up to their promises and hype."

The strategy, decoded

What thesis is Rogo betting on, whose job it automates first, why the changelog ships in exactly this order, and what the IB-only bet buys and costs.

Reverse-engineered product strategy and prioritization logic

The bet: sell the deliverable, not the chat

Rogo is not selling a smarter analyst chatbot. It is selling an auditable, banker-shaped output: a cited cell, a structurally correct model, a revised slide. In finance the constraint on adoption is not capability, it is trust, and the deliverable is the unit that carries it. Three ship decisions give the bet away:

  • Citations are the headline, not a footnote. In-cell citations shipped as a marquee feature in Nov 2025, then extended across text and sheets. A consumer AI product buries citations; Rogo leads with them. That ordering only makes sense if the buyer's first objection is "I can't put an unsourced number in front of a client."
  • They sell conformance to craft, not raw horsepower. The Sheets Agent's stated differentiator is that it is "built like a banker would," with "clean structure and proper conventions." The pitch is fidelity to how bankers already work, not a higher benchmark score.
  • The moat is domain scaffolding plus licensed data, not a base model. Rogo does not pretrain its own foundation model. It fine-tunes third-party models, routes between them with a published Model Broker, and layers finance-specific scaffolding, citations, and the S&P Capital IQ, FactSet, and filings corpus on top. The wager is that generic frontier models are not trusted or shaped enough for the domain, so defensibility is meant to live in the tuning, the harness, and the data rights rather than the weights.

Who gets automated first: the modeling grunt work of a sell-side desk

The ICP is the junior deal-team professional: the analyst or associate on a sell-side or advisory team, adjacent into PE and equity research. The Jan-2026 Projects copy names the desk directly: "Running a sell-side process? You need instant access to your financial model, call notes, marketing materials, and legal documents."

The wedge is the model and the comps, and it is well chosen for three converging reasons that most wedges only hit one of:

  • High-volume and repetitive, so the ROI story writes itself.
  • Objectively checkable. A model ties out or it does not, so trust can be demonstrated rather than asserted. Rogo picked the one analyst task where correctness is falsifiable.
  • Portable into artifacts bankers already live in, which explains the Nov-2025 investment in copy-paste fidelity and PPTX / Word / PDF export.

The strategic move underneath: automate the drudgery, leave the judgment and the client relationship with the human. That is how you get adopted instead of resisted. Note the split between user and buyer: the user is the analyst, but the load-bearing features (SharePoint as a firm-native source, Projects as a shared dataroom, licensed S&P data "with the appropriate agreement in place") are all firm-level. Land on the analyst, expand to the firm.

Sequencing: why answers, then deliverables, then workspace, then embed

The changelog is a trust-then-scope ladder, and the order is the argument:

  1. Aug–Sep 2025, reasoning first (Rogo Pro, four-model lineup). Prove the answers are good enough for finance before touching artifacts.
  2. Oct 2025, deepen the moat, cut friction (S&P Capital IQ, Prompt Optimizer, shortcuts, dark mode). License the proprietary data and make the tool a daily driver.
  3. Nov–Dec 2025, the deliverable layer (multi-tab Sheets Agent, in-cell citations, PDF/PPTX/Word export, AI Tables on proprietary models). Now the output is both trustworthy and client-ready on exit.
  4. Jan 2026, the workspace (Projects). Only once single deliverables are trusted do they introduce persistent multi-file, multi-chat context: the container for a whole deal.
  5. May 2026, meet users where they work (Excel plugin, Memory, Slide Annotations). Stop dragging bankers into Rogo; put Felix inside Excel and PowerPoint.

The governing logic: you cannot orchestrate a deal you are not yet trusted to do a single cell of. Citations and banker-grade structure are the price of admission, so they ship first. Deal orchestration sits at the top of the value pyramid, and Rogo is climbing trusted output → trusted workspace → (eventual) trusted process. Selling orchestration before trust would have failed the MD sniff test.

Where the puck is going

  • From answers to system of record. Projects plus Memory plus SharePoint-as-source is Rogo quietly becoming where a deal lives, not where you ask questions. Memory ("your role, formatting rules, the companies you work with most") is the seed of an agent that anticipates the work.
  • From "come to Rogo" to "Rogo everywhere." The Excel plugin and Slide Annotations are a distribution bet: embed in the two apps bankers cannot leave, make the incumbents the surface and Felix the brain.
  • Toward proactive multi-step agents, but not yet in the shipped app. Felix as a named persona, plus Memory, plus multi-tab autonomy, is agent scaffolding. Projects is the deal container that orchestration would run inside. But the captured app ships no real orchestration; Deal Room, CRM, and Agents are backend-less prototypes. That gap is the real tell: they have laid the runway and are pacing the aircraft behind trust.
  • The story has already moved to autopilot, even if the product has not. Rogo's public narrative pivoted over the last year from copilot to autonomous agent. The Series D language claims Felix "executes complex, multi-step financial processes autonomously, from deal screening and CIM generation to buyer outreach and data room diligence," that you email it like a colleague, and that it works asynchronously. The marketing is where the puck is going; the shipped, tested experience is still catching up, which is the single most useful tension in this teardown.
  • Widening from sell-side to buy-side. The marketed workflows now include Financial Sponsor Overview and Secondaries Buyer Overview, and Rogo has extended toward private equity and hedge funds. This is them reaching for a bigger market than sell-side IB alone, which is the natural answer to the vertical-ceiling problem, though competitors argue the sell-side template rigidity makes them weak at buy-side deep diligence.

GTM and buyer signals

The assignment's specific enterprise artifacts (RBAC, SCIM, SSO, admin consent, granular sharing, Metronome billing) do not appear anywhere in this changelog, so I do not assert them. What the ship record does reveal:

  • Land-and-expand, analyst-in and firm-out. Individual productivity wins (Prompt Optimizer, shortcuts, dark mode) sit right next to firm-level commitments (SharePoint connection, licensed S&P data "requiring the appropriate agreement"). The individual adopts; the firm signs the data and integration contracts.
  • "Firm-native" is the enterprise wedge. SharePoint as a searchable, cited source ("CIMs, pitch decks, IC memos, diligence materials") requires an admin to connect the tenant. That connection is the moment the deal turns from seat to platform.
  • Data licensing as co-sell. The S&P Capital IQ integration ("available to S&P Global clients with the appropriate agreement") points to a partner-leveraged motion: Rogo rides the firm's existing data relationships and makes itself the interface to data the firm already pays for.
  • Inferred buyer map. Economic buyer is firm leadership, a COO or a coverage-group head who owns both the data agreements and the "make my analysts faster" ROI. Champion and user is the analyst. A security/IT stakeholder is implied by the SharePoint tenant connection but is not evidenced with any named RBAC/SSO/SCIM feature in this artifact.

Where the strategy is coherent, and where it strains

Coherent. Trust-first sequencing holds end to end. "Meet users in Excel and PowerPoint" is consistent with a workflow-embed strategy rather than rip-and-replace. Proprietary reasoning plus licensed data is a self-reinforcing moat that reinforces the trust thesis rather than sitting beside it.

Strained. Four seams are worth naming:

  • The router contradicts the "Felix just handles it" story. They ship a four-model selector (Instant / Fast / Pro / Deep Research), a Ctrl+\ toggle, and a Prompt Optimizer, while also shipping a "simpler home page." They are asking the user to make routing and prompting decisions while claiming to remove that burden. A fully confident agent auto-routes; exposing the router is a hedge.
  • An uncited fast mode inside a citations-obsessed product. The "Instant" tier is GPT-5-only with no sources. A fast, uncitable mode is philosophically at odds with "every number is traceable." Defensible in practice, but a seam in the narrative.
  • Defensibility sold, dependency shipped. Rogo sells finance-shaped reasoning as the moat, yet every tier ultimately runs on OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, with no model of its own. Pragmatic, and the Model Broker makes a virtue of it, but it is the exposed dependency independent analysts keep naming for a company that markets defensibility.
  • Two competing surface strategies. "Come live in Rogo Projects" and "stay in Excel and PowerPoint" both claim to own deal context. Reconcilable for now, but they will eventually have to decide which surface is the system of record or risk fragmenting it.

Prioritization logic, decoded

  • Ship proof-of-trust before scope. An unauditable number is unusable in finance, so citations and banker-grade structure lead.
  • Wedge on the most delegatable, most checkable task. Modeling and comps: high-volume, objectively right-or-wrong, low political resistance.
  • Answers → deliverables → workspace → embed, in that order. Prove reasoning, then produce artifacts, then persist context, then go to where users already work.
  • License the moat data early. S&P Capital IQ plus SharePoint-as-source makes Rogo firm-native and rides existing enterprise data agreements: moat and GTM in one move.
  • Land on the analyst, expand to the firm. Individual-productivity features drive adoption; tenant integrations and data agreements convert it to a platform sale.
  • Pace orchestration behind trust. Projects is the container for orchestration, shipped before any orchestration exists. Build the runway, wait until trust is banked.
  • Cut input friction, not just output friction. Prompt Optimizer, Memory, and Slide Annotations all lower the cost of asking, on the bet that adoption dies on prompt-anxiety as much as on answer quality.
  • Distribute by embedding in incumbents. Excel plugin plus Slide Annotations make PowerPoint and Excel the surface and Felix the brain, rather than fighting for a new destination app.

The IB-only bet: what verticalization buys, and what it costs

Rogo did not build a finance assistant. It built an investment-banking assistant, and the choice of "only" is a strategy in itself, with a payoff and a bill.

What the vertical buys

  • A legal moat a horizontal tool cannot cross. The licensed-data bench (S&P Capital IQ, FactSet, PitchBook, Preqin, Moody's, Third Bridge, GuidePoint) plus VDR access to Ideals and Intralinks are negotiated entitlements, not features. A general assistant cannot reach a firm's Capital IQ seats or a live data room no matter how good its model. Going vertical is what earns the paper.
  • Concentration of willingness-to-pay. One buyer set, one procurement path, one compliance story. Banks pay for tools that clear infosec and put a cited number in front of a client, and Rogo built exactly to that bar. A horizontal tool has to re-earn trust in every vertical it touches; Rogo earns it once.
  • The right to encode the craft. "Built like a banker would" is only a differentiator if you serve one kind of banker. Format fidelity, the circularity switch in the expected place, the DRL that matches the workstream, all of it is possible because the surface area is one industry's conventions rather than everyone's.

What the vertical costs

  • A hard TAM ceiling. Investment banking is on the order of a few hundred thousand front-office advisory and M&A seats worldwide (the deal-team population, not the broader capital-markets headcount), not millions. If Rogo prices to the analyst (which its roadmap implies), seat count is bounded and average contract value is capped, while the data COGS underneath (S&P, FactSet, Third Bridge) is heavy and largely fixed. A narrow, low-priced seat base has to carry an expensive data bill. This is the economic argument for climbing to the senior seat: the MD is the one axis where willingness-to-pay is not capped.
  • Cyclicality with no hedge. Seat revenue moves with deal volume. When M&A freezes, banks cut seats and squeeze budgets at the same time, and an IB-only vendor takes both hits at once. A horizontal AI product is diversified across sectors that do not all contract together; Rogo is levered to the deal cycle by construction.
  • The moat is also a cage. The same specialization that blocks horizontal tools from coming in blocks Rogo from going out. Expanding into an adjacent market is a rebuild, not a toggle, because the format encoding, the data licenses, and the workflows are all IB-shaped. And the moat rents concentration risk: a handful of data vendors hold the entitlements, and any one of them can renegotiate terms or build the interface themselves.
  • Encroachment on the exact layer Rogo occupies. Rogo planted its flag on analyst execution, which is the most model-substitutable work in the building. Every frontier-model release erodes it, and a bulge bracket with an internal AI-platform team can rebuild the orchestration and system prompts over Azure OpenAI or Bedrock and license the same data directly. The commoditizable layer is precisely where Rogo is strongest and least defensible.

The escape, and why it doubles the senior thesis

There are two ways out of the ceiling. Raise willingness-to-pay by climbing to the senior seat, the MD who originates and signs rather than the analyst who builds. Or raise seat count by expanding into the adjacent markets that share the same data and the same deal-shaped work: private equity, equity research, private credit, corporate development. Rogo's own copy already hints at the second ("adjacent into PE and equity research"), and its prototype backlog (a CRM, institutional memory, a deal room) is the infrastructure for the first.

The trap is doing neither. IB-only and analyst-only compounds the ceiling: the smallest seat count, times the lowest willingness-to-pay, times the most commoditizable work. That is the corner Rogo has shipped itself into, and it is the corner a senior-first challenger is built to exploit.

The Good

What Rogo does genuinely well, and why each strength is hard for a general tool to copy.

Where Rogo Is Genuinely Strong

The licensed-data and deal-room moat

Rogo has wired in a bench of separately-licensed sources most AI-native startups will never assemble: S&P Capital IQ (Ownership, Transactions, Company Intelligence), FactSet, PitchBook, Preqin, Moody's, LSEG, plus expert-network content from Third Bridge and GuidePoint, and firm-native M365 and Salesforce.

The clone-resistant part is not the engineering, it is the paper. Each of these is a negotiated commercial license with its own entitlement and redistribution terms. A general assistant cannot legally reach a firm's Capital IQ or Third Bridge entitlements no matter how capable the model. This is the one strength even direct rivals concede: F2's own comparison page calls Rogo's data-partnership network best in class and Rogo "the leading AI research platform for Wall Street."

The sharpest edge is the VDR connection: Ideals and Intralinks integration means "document Q&A across deal rooms you have access to." That puts Rogo inside live diligence, not just public market data, which is where the highest-value execution work actually happens. This is a business-development and legal achievement competitors have to re-earn deal by deal, not a feature they can ship.

Citations land in the cell, not the footnote

In-cell citations (Nov 2025) carry through the Dec 2025 Sheets Agent into multi-tab models. Model tiers are source-scoped: Instant touches no external sources, Fast and Pro use all connected data.

The insight is placement. A reviewing associate clicks a driver inside the model and traces it to the filing it came from, which is exactly the motion a VP or MD uses to check work. A prose answer with footnotes fails a staffing review; a model where every cell is traceable survives one. That is the difference between an interesting answer and a defensible deliverable.

The output is shaped like a banker's, not a chatbot's

The Sheets Agent builds multi-tab models "built like a banker would" and auto-spawns a spreadsheet when it detects modeling intent. Rogo bought this capability rather than only building it, acquiring Subset in September 2025 to get a spreadsheet agent that reads, audits, and edits the sprawling forty-tab valuation models bankers actually live in. The built-in prompt library encodes the actual job: 3-statement models with circularity switches, revolvers and cases, DCFs with sensitivity and CAPM/WACC, LBO MOIC decomposition, SOTP and break-up analyses, convertible bond models.

Bankers do not want a novel spreadsheet shape; they want the conventional structure they already review, with the switches in the places they expect. Encoding those format conventions, not just the math, is what collapses adoption friction, because the reviewer's existing muscle memory still works. A horizontal model produces correct numbers in an unfamiliar layout, which is exactly the thing a bank rejects.

The model router is a published strategy, not just a menu

A four-tier selector (Instant, Fast, Pro, Deep Research) sits over a router Rogo publicly calls the Model Broker, spanning frontier models from every major lab (gpt-5.1, gemini-3, claude-opus-4.8, and more in the bundles), with per-model admin gates (ModelOpus4, ModelGpt5, ModelGemini3). Worth being precise about what is under it: Rogo does not pretrain a proprietary foundation model. Felix is a finance-specific agent harness plus fine-tuned third-party models, and the routing is the product. Rogo even publishes its own eval, the Big Finance Benchmark, and uses its headline result (no single frontier model wins, the best scores under 60 percent) to justify routing rather than betting on one model.

Two things make this durable. The tiers map to the decision a user actually has, latency versus depth, rather than a model-name choice they cannot rank. And routing across providers insulates Rogo from any single vendor's price hike, outage, or capability regression, and lets it put the cheapest adequate model on each job. The margin structure and the reliability story both improve as the model market churns. The cost is transparency: the user is never told which model answered, and the honest downside, which competitors press on, is that a router over other people's models is the ingredient a well-resourced bank can most easily reproduce.

Export meets the deliverable where it is judged

Native export to PowerPoint, Word, PDF and Excel, plus rich copy-paste (Nov 2025) and, from May 2026, an Excel taskpane where Felix edits models in place and slide annotations where it revises a PPTX in place.

Banking output is judged as a clean PPT or a working Excel model, so the tool has to leave its own walls cleanly. The taskpane is the strongest version: Felix operates inside the file the associate already lives in rather than forcing a round-trip through a web app. Meeting the deliverable in its native format is a quiet but real adoption lever, and a gap for any tool that only emits text.

Enterprise trust that clears procurement

The security surface is deep: SSO through enterprise IdPs, SCIM provisioning, idle and session-lifespan controls with forced logout, an audit log filterable by user, action and client IP, DLP-style blocked-words and regex controls, tenant-level connector consent for M365, and a real authorization model in ChatRbac plus granular share scopes across Chat, Project, Skill and Note.

This is the checklist that clears a bank's infosec review, and most AI-native startups fail it. The audit log with client IP answers the compliance question that matters, who asked the model what. The one caveat worth stating: all of it is org-admin scoped and SSO-dependent. It gets Rogo through procurement, but a senior individual user sees and controls none of their own security posture, so it never becomes a felt trust surface for the person actually using the product.

The pattern to take away. Rogo's real strengths cluster precisely where a general tool cannot follow: entitled data, live deal-room access, banker-format encoding, and procurement-grade trust. These are slow and expensive to replicate and nearly invisible in a demo, which is the point. Its capabilities are strongest exactly where they are hardest to see, and weakest at telling the individual senior user why any of it matters to them. For a platform aimed at that senior user, those are the strengths to respect and the weakness to build against.

The Bad

Where Rogo is genuinely exposed as a product today, and who each weakness hurts most.

Rogo Weaknesses: Where It Is Exposed

1. The product moves cognitive load upstream instead of removing it

Everything in Rogo waits for a prompt. The two entry points are "What can Felix do for you?" and "Ask a question" (Section E). The only proactive surface, Scheduled Tasks, is user-configured recurring prompts, not a guided workflow (Section E). The brief's own verdict: cognitive load is not reduced, it is relocated to prompt-authoring (Section E).

The Prompt Optimizer ships that concession in code. In October 2025 Rogo added a one-click query rewriter (Section A). You only build a tool to fix the user's prompt if the raw prompt bar is too hard to use. It patches the symptom and leaves the disease.

This tax falls hardest on the persona Rogo already underserves. The built-in prompts are analyst execution deliverables (Section H), so an MD who originates and judges rather than builds has to sit at a query bar iterating phrasing to get anything back. The contrast we can draw is direct: agents run the repeatable process, the banker does not sit composing prompts.

2. Felix hides which model answered, which breaks defensibility

The bundles carry a router of frontier models: gpt-5.1, o3, gemini-3-pro, claude-opus-4.8, claude-fable-5, plus Rogo's own model, none disclosed to the user in the UI (Section B). Felix is a brand over that router, not a single model, and the UI never tells the user which model answered or why Felix differs from using Opus 4.8 directly (Section B).

For a banker, provenance is liability. Anyone signing off on a number, in IC prep, on a commitment committee, in risk, is personally attesting to how it was produced, and cannot say whether it came from a frontier model or a cheaper routed fallback. The gap also invites the lethal question the brief names: if a user can reach Opus 4.8 themselves, why pay for a wrapper that hides which model is answering? A visible "answered by [model]" line turns Rogo's opacity into someone else's feature.

3. There is no per-user spend meter, only an admin credit console

Rogo has the billing plumbing and chooses not to point it at the user. CostGuard polls cost every five minutes and, on block, disables the ask bar (Section D). The Credit Center is real but is a beta, org-admin tab denominated in opaque credits, not tokens or dollars (Section D). A getMyUsage endpoint exists but is gated behind UsageV2 (Section D). The net, in the brief's words: no transparent, always-on, per-user, dollar or token spend tracker for a senior professional (Section D).

Three failures compound. The person spending has no live view of their own consumption. Credits make dollars-per-deliverable unknowable. And the first signal of a cap can be a dead input box mid-task, with the user eating the cost of the opacity. A senior professional cannot set a personal ceiling, watch burn against it, or price a deliverable before running it. An always-on per-user dollar meter answers all three, and Rogo has nothing shipped like it.

4. Ten months of roadmap went to the analyst, not the buyer

Rogo's entire shipping history is analyst and associate deliverable-automation: models, decks, tables, citations, exports (Section A). The built-in prompts confirm the target: 3-statement models, DCFs, LBO MOIC decomposition, SOTP, PIB generation, DRLs, all associate execution work (Section H). The product is optimized for the person building the model, not the MD originating, judging, and running the deal; senior workflows like origination, relationship intelligence, IC prep, and risk sign-off are thin or absent (Section H).

That is a hard commitment to reverse, and it caps Rogo commercially. It sells into the associate pool but never becomes indispensable to the decision-makers who hold the budget. The highest-value, highest-willingness-to-pay seat in the bank is the one Rogo's product does not serve, which is exactly the seat a senior-built challenger takes.

5. Rogo has broadcast its roadmap and shipped none of the backend

The proactive future is mocked, not built. Deal Room is a prototype with no backend and seeded data only, employee-gated (Section E). The CRM and institutional-memory surface and the Agents surface are the same: prototypes, no backend, seeded data (Section E). There is no start-a-mandate flow that runs the workstreams for you (Section E). The brief reads it plainly: Rogo internally knows the future is proactive deal-room orchestration plus institutional memory and has not shipped a backend for any of it (Section E).

This is the sharpest opening, and it is now compounded by the marketing. Rogo's public story has moved to autopilot: the Series D language claims Felix "executes complex, multi-step financial processes autonomously, from deal screening and CIM generation to buyer outreach and data room diligence." But the shipped app a real bank tested is still reactive chat, and Ignatious ran it for three weeks, judged it weak, and reverted to their existing PowerPoint tool. The gap between the autonomous-agent claim and the pilot experience is the credibility risk a governed, actually-proactive product exploits directly.

6. "Workspace" is folders around the same reactive chat

Projects is marketed as a workspace: folders of chats, persistent instructions, a project library (Section A). Underneath, the interaction is still the ask bar and everything still waits for a prompt (Section E). Memory, added May 2026, stores role and formatting conventions passively in Settings; it remembers preferences, it does not act on the deal (Section A).

Workspace language implies an environment that carries and advances the work. What ships is a folder around reactive chat plus a preference store. That gap between frame and product is a credibility wedge in a procurement trial, and it lands hardest on the senior buyer who bought the promise that the tool would reduce the work rather than reshuffle it.

These do not stand alone, they stack. The core is reactive (1), the proactive surface is unbuilt (5), and the workspace framing oversells both (6). The opening is a real guided-deal engine with visible model provenance and a per-user spend meter (2, 3), aimed at the senior origination-and-judgment seat (4) that Rogo's analyst-first roadmap leaves open.

05

The Ugly

The deeper structural risks. Rogo shipped the commoditizable layer and does not own the one part that is hard to copy.

The Ugly: Structural and Strategic Risks

1. "Felix" is a router, and the router is the commodity

Felix is not a model. The bundle exposes it as a brand and orchestration layer over a fleet of frontier engines (gpt-5.1, o3, gemini-3-pro, claude-opus-4.8, claude-fable-5, qwen, glm, a Cerebras experiment), and the public record confirms there is no proprietary foundation model underneath: Rogo does not pretrain its own model, it fine-tunes third-party ones and routes between them with what it calls the Model Broker. So the shipped product resolves to: take a prompt, pick a frontier model, wrap it in finance system prompts and connectors, return a deliverable.

None of those ingredients is durably defensible. Orchestration and system prompts are rebuildable by any bank that already licenses Azure OpenAI and Bedrock and staffs an internal AI platform team, which the bulge brackets now do. Connector depth is licensable directly from the same data vendors. And the model itself belongs to OpenAI and Google, which is exactly the attack independent analysts already make: Sacra calls the dependence on third-party models a core structural vulnerability, and AlphaSense goes further, calling Rogo "completely reliant on OpenAI's models, which are infamous for their propensity to hallucinate." Rogo has productized the layer a well-resourced customer can most easily reproduce, and it does not own the one part that is hard to copy.

What this means for us: do not counter-route to frontier models. Compete on what a router cannot copy. Own connector-and-permissioning integration so deep that an internal rebuild is a multi-year project, and encode how deals actually run rather than how models answer. Rogo's own prototype backlog (Deal Room, CRM, Agents) shows they already know orchestration alone will not hold.

2. Hiding the model forfeits the only argument that beats "just use Claude directly"

The UI never discloses which engine answered or why Felix beats calling Opus 4.8 directly. That opacity is not a cosmetic gap, it is strategically self-defeating. The shipped deliverables (3-statement models, DCFs, LBO MOIC decomposition) are exactly the analyst-execution tasks a frontier model does well unaided, so the customer's rational question is what the wrapper adds over a subscription that costs a fraction of a Rogo seat.

Hiding the model destroys the one answer that would justify the markup: a benchmarked quality delta on financial tasks ("on this work, Felix beats raw Opus 4.8 by X"). You cannot charge for a delta you refuse to show. In a room of skeptical MDs, "trust us, it is better, we will not tell you how" reads as hiding weakness.

What this means for us: make model transparency and a published financial-task benchmark a selling surface. Show which engine ran, why the router chose it, and the measured lift over calling that engine naked. Turn the customer's most dangerous question into the pitch.

3. Real security, built and then hidden, in the one vertical bought on trust

The security engineering is genuine: SSO, SCIM, idle and session controls, an IP-stamped audit log, DLP-style blocked words and regex, connector admin consent, attestations. Then it is buried. There is no native app-level 2FA (twoFactor, totp, saml all return zero in the bundle); MFA is entirely delegated to enterprise SSO; every control is org-admin scoped; and nothing is surfaced to the individual user as a trust surface.

This is the worst of both outcomes. Rogo paid for the security and collects none of the perceived-trust dividend in the vertical where trust is bought by the person in the room, not a SOC 2 PDF in a data room. Two failures follow. A senior reviewer who pokes the app sees no visible second factor and no personal security surface and concludes it is not built for them, even though the org controls exist. And any user or firm without enterprise SSO has literally no second factor, an unforced hole in the one vertical where a single breach ends the company. For a challenger aimed at senior individuals and smaller firms rather than SSO-federated bulge brackets, that is not an edge case, it is the core segment.

What this means for us: ship trust as a product surface. Native app-level 2FA and passkeys that do not require the firm to run SSO. A per-user security center: active sessions, device list, per-connector data-access scoping, and lineage for what Felix saw and where it went.

4. The differentiated future is three prototypes with no backend

Everything shipped is the commoditizable analyst-execution layer. Everything defensible is a mock. Deal Room, the CRM ("Institutional Intelligence"), and the Agents prototype are all employee-gated, seeded-data demos with no backend. Rogo has diagrammed its own endgame (proactive deal-room orchestration, institutional memory, agents) and shipped none of it.

Three risks compound. If these mocks leak into sales as "coming soon," Rogo is selling a roadmap to a buyer set that punishes vaporware. Building the real backend (a deal-room orchestrator, a CRM with institutional memory) is a large systems build measured in quarters to years, not a changelog line. And by leaving the blueprint in a shipped bundle, Rogo has told every competitor exactly what it believes the endgame is.

What this means for us: build the backend Rogo is only mocking, aim it at the senior user Rogo is not serving, and reach GA on the defensible layer while Rogo is still shipping export-to-PowerPoint. The window is the gap between Rogo's prototype and Rogo's GA. Whoever ships the real deal-room-and-institutional-memory backend first owns the category's actual moat.

5. Opaque "credits" are a margin instrument a financial buyer will read as a spread

Cost infrastructure exists but is beta, org-admin scoped, and denominated in credits rather than tokens or dollars. There is no always-on, per-user, dollar-denominated spend tracker. Combined with model opacity, the user can see neither which model ran nor what it actually cost, while the billing rails (Metronome) are usage-based, so real cost varies by model and that variance is abstracted away.

Credits are a margin instrument: they decouple what the user pays from what the underlying call costs, capturing spread and smoothing COGS. Against a naive audience that is ordinary SaaS pricing. Against this audience it is a liability, because pricing opaque spreads and undisclosed markups is what bankers do for a living. A senior user who works out that "credits" hide both the model and the cost reads it as being played, and the reaction is sharper than from a normal SaaS user. It also makes ROI impossible to reason about, which cuts straight into the budget-holder conversation below.

What this means for us: radical cost legibility. Show the model, the tokens, and the dollar cost per query and per deal, and let a senior user set a personal budget in dollars. Framed in their own terms: you would never accept an undisclosed spread from a counterparty, so we do not hand you one.

6. Rogo is priced and positioned to the person who does not hold the budget

Ten months of shipping is overwhelmingly analyst and associate deliverable-automation: models, decks, tables, citations, exports. The built-in prompts confirm it, and the missing senior workflows are named by their absence: origination, relationship intelligence, IC and commitment-committee prep, live negotiation support, coverage monitoring, judgment and risk sign-off. Cognitive load is not reduced, only relocated to prompt-authoring.

The economics of that choice are unfavorable. Analysts execute; MDs and deal teams decide what to buy and sign the invoice. A faster analyst is real value but caps willingness-to-pay at "what is a faster analyst worth," and the champion is not the economic buyer. Worse, analyst execution is the most model-substitutable work in the building, so it sits directly under the commoditization pressure from points 1 and 2. Rogo has planted its flag on the most contested, most commoditizable, lowest-willingness-to-pay square, and left the high-willingness-to-pay senior square, which is also the defensible one, as unshipped prototypes.

What this means for us: sell to the MD, price to the mandate. Build the senior surface (origination and coverage intelligence, IC prep, live negotiation support, a real institutional-memory CRM) and, above all, a guided deal rail where agents run the repeatable workstreams and the banker keeps the judgment, orchestration that removes cognitive load on the repeatable work instead of relocating it to prompt-authoring. That buyer holds the budget, carries orders-of-magnitude higher willingness-to-pay, and wants judgment leverage a raw frontier model cannot give, which escapes the commoditization trap and the pricing ceiling at once.

The pattern

Rogo shipped the commoditizable layer and turned each of its three defensible assets into a liability by hiding it. It hid the model, which invites both commoditization and the "just use Claude" threat. It hid the security, forfeiting trust in the vertical that buys on trust. It hid the cost behind credits, a margin move a skeptical financial buyer resents. And it has not built the differentiated future at all, while aiming the whole product at the analyst rather than the budget-holding MD. Every hidden strength is an opening. Transparency of model, cost, and security, plus a shipped senior deal-orchestration backend, is a single coherent wedge straight through the middle of what Rogo has left undefended.

06

The senior-IB gap analysis

Rogo automates the analyst. Here is everything the Managing Director actually needs that the analyst tool leaves untouched.

Senior-IB gap analysis: what MDs actually need that Rogo does not build

Rogo automates the analyst: it answers questions and produces work products (models, profiles, slides). But a Managing Director does not spend the day answering questions. They decide which relationships to work, which deals to staff, which risks to sign under, and which calls to make this week. Their output is judgment, not deliverables. Every gap below is the same fault line viewed from a different angle: Rogo tools the analyst's keystrokes and leaves the senior banker's actual job untouched.

1. Origination and relationship intelligence

An MD's franchise is a book of 150 to 400 living relationships across CEOs, CFOs, sponsors, and board members. Their edge is knowing that a CFO they covered three years ago just landed at a new portfolio company, that a sponsor is nearing end-of-fund-life and has to exit two names, that a competitor just lost a mandate they can take. That is pattern recognition over a relationship graph crossed with time and market events.

Gap. Rogo is query-driven: it answers what you ask about a company or filing. It holds no model of your relationships, no memory of who you cover, no read on the ownership, fund-life, and management-change signals that trigger a call. It cannot tell you who to call Monday morning. Origination is the highest-margin thing a senior banker does, and it is exactly where Rogo is silent.

2. Coverage monitoring that pushes

A group head owns a sector and needs to know, without asking, when a covered name pre-announces, when a comp re-rates, when a sponsor files an S-1, when covenants tighten across the book. The trigger has to arrive while they are in a meeting, not when they next think to run a query.

Gap. Rogo is pull, not push. No standing watch on a defined coverage universe, no alerting engine, no "here is what moved in your names overnight and why it matters to you." An analyst tool waits to be opened; a senior tool reaches out first. Rogo has no proactive surface at all.

3. Live deal-process orchestration

In an active deal the MD runs six workstreams at once: diligence, legal, financing, QoE, buyer list, data room. They need the live state of the process, who is behind, which requests are open, whether the debt commitment is tracking, what breaks if the timeline slips a week. They run the deal; they do not build the model.

Gap. Rogo produces artifacts, not process. It has no concept of a deal as a stateful, multi-workstream object with dependencies and owners, so it cannot say "financing is three days behind and that puts signing at risk." Deliverable automation is not orchestration.

4. Committee prep and the adversarial pass

Before capital or the firm's name goes out, the deal faces a committee. The sponsor has to anticipate the three questions that sink it, pressure-test the downside, and find where the analysis is thin before a skeptical partner does. Writing the memo is the easy part; defending it is the job.

Gap. Rogo can help draft the memo. It will not play the skeptic, generate the questions the committee will ask, or hunt for the weakest assumption in its own output. It optimizes for producing the document, not for surviving the room the document walks into.

5. Negotiation and term-sheet support

When the term sheet is live the MD trades points: leverage, equity cheque, reps, earn-out, break fee. They need to see, in real time, what each concession costs in returns, what precedent says is market, and where the counterparty's leverage actually sits. Live, adversarial, numeric.

Gap. Rogo has no negotiation surface. It does not sensitize a term against the return, hold a book of market precedents to anchor a position, or sit beside the banker while points move. It answers questions about a company; it does not help win a room.

6. Judgment isolation and risk sign-off

The senior banker's signature is the product. Putting the firm's name on a fairness opinion, a valuation range, or a recommendation stakes reputation and license. They need to know precisely where the judgment calls sit, which assumptions carry the range, and what they are personally vouching for.

Gap. Rogo presents outputs as answers, not as a stack of judgment calls with owners. Nothing isolates "here are the three assumptions your name rests on, who set each, and the range if each is wrong." It automates the analyst's work and leaves the person who signs under it unequipped.

7. Sign-off-grade auditability

Every number that leaves the building must trace to a source under a partner's name, defensible to a client, a regulator, or opposing counsel. "The AI said so" is not a citation a partner can stand behind in a boardroom, so today a human still checks the machine's work.

Gap. Rogo removes the analyst's keystrokes but not the partner's liability. There is no sign-off-grade audit layer: every figure sourced, every assumption flagged, every step reproducible to the standard a partner can personally attest to. Analyst-grade output still demands analyst-grade checking, and Rogo leaves that on the human.

8. Delegation with approval gates

A senior banker manages by delegation. They want to hand a task off the way they hand it to a VP: "build the buyer list, flag anything odd, come back to me before it goes out." That means bounded autonomy with checkpoints, not a chat window they babysit turn by turn.

Gap. Rogo is an interactive assistant: it takes a turn, waits, takes another. There is no model where the MD assigns a multi-step task, sets the gates, and lets the agent run to a gate and stop. Senior people delegate; they do not prompt. Rogo requires prompting.

9. The accountability cockpit

An MD running an AI across live deals needs a control panel: what is it doing in my name right now, what did it touch this week, what did it cost, what is walled off, who else can see it. Under information-barrier and MNPI rules that is not a convenience, it is a control requirement.

Gap. Rogo is a workspace, not a cockpit. There is no personal view of agent activity, spend, data access, and information-barrier state from the seat of the person accountable for all of it. The banker owns what the AI did and has no dashboard that shows him what that was.

The through-line

Rogo automates the analyst. A senior banker's day is relationships, process, negotiation, and accountability, and none of those is a work product. The analyst asks "what's the answer?" The MD asks "who do I call, which deal do I staff, what am I signing, and what is my AI doing in my name?" Rogo answers the first question and is silent on the other three.

The point of this section is not to pitch a product. It is to line up each thing Rogo built against the approach we are taking, so the good, the bad, and the ugly of Rogo turn into concrete build decisions for a platform aimed at senior investment bankers. Where our own thinking has already moved (the vault model, the rails-and-skills split, proactive compliance, the deal rail Roberto has in a PR), the comparison shows why.

One framing correction up front. The goal is not a system that "runs the deal." That phrase scares buyers and it is the wrong claim. The banker keeps the judgment. What we automate is the repeatable, low-judgment work that consumes the day, and we do it proactively instead of waiting to be prompted. Proactive on the repeatable, human on the judgment. That reconciles the cognitive-load complaint with the reality that no MD will hand a machine the decisions their name sits behind.

Guided deal workflow versus reactive chat

What Rogo does. Everything waits for a prompt. The two entry points are "What can Felix do for you?" and "Ask a question." The proactive future (Deal Room, an institutional-memory CRM, an Agents surface) exists only as employee-gated prototypes with no backend and seeded data. The Prompt Optimizer is the tell: you only build a tool to rewrite the user's prompt if the blank prompt bar is too hard to use. The result is that cognitive load is not removed, it is relocated to prompt-authoring. Worth remembering that Ignatious ran Rogo for three weeks, found it weak, and reverted to their existing PowerPoint tool. The read on Rogo's real value to that bank was liability insurance, AI credibility, and junior-banker retention, not output quality.

Our approach. A guided deal rail, which is what Roberto already has in PR #62. A mandate begins with a short setup (assign a project code name, set team and permissions, notify compliance, mark where in the lifecycle the deal sits), and each step can be skipped, assigned to a person, or assigned to an agent, with a deadline or priority tag. From there the rail advances on its own through the repeatable steps and stops at human gates for anything that carries judgment. This is the rails-and-skills split: the rails (orchestration, permissions, approval gates, audit log, cost attribution, pre-send checks) are ours; the deal-specific skills sit on top. The MVP deliberately covers only the repeatable, lower-judgment steps. PowerPoint generation, financial modeling, and complex valuation are explicitly out of v1, which is the opposite of where Rogo spent its last ten months.

Model transparency and choice

What Rogo does. Felix is a brand over a router of frontier models plus one proprietary financial-reasoning model, confirmed both in the bundles and by inspection: it is a proprietary router, not a single model, not built on a Chinese open-source model, and Fable 5 is not in the lineup. The router itself is a reasonable hedge (the tiers map to latency versus depth, and routing across providers protects margin and uptime). The failure is that the UI never tells the user which model answered, and there is no way to reason about why Felix would beat calling a frontier model directly.

Our approach. Name the model. Show which engine answered and let the user see it. For v1 we point at a single Anthropic model through the enterprise API rather than multi-model routing, because a clean, single-model audit trail matters more for a compliance product than squeezing cost, and banks are already comfortable with Anthropic. Multi-model orchestration is a v2 decision once the audit story is airtight. This directly answers the "why not just use Claude" question that Rogo cannot: the value is the governed rails, the deal memory, and the compliance layer around the model, not a mystery wrapper over it.

Security, two-factor, and the vault

What Rogo does well. More than the marketing admits. Deep org security exists (SSO, SCIM, an audit log stamped with client IP, DLP-style blocked words and regex, session timeouts). It does not train on your content, connectors are read-only OAuth scoped to what your account can already see, SharePoint is retrieved at query time rather than indexed, and processing runs over US labs (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google).

What Rogo does badly. There is no native two-factor authentication. This is not a code-search abstraction: logging in on Seth's account, there was no prompt to set up 2FA and no way to turn it on in settings. All the real security is org-admin scoped and SSO-dependent, so an individual or a smaller firm without SSO gets no second factor at all, and no user-facing trust surface. You also cannot bulk-delete history, and there is nothing keeping the tool inside its lane (it was happy to plan a fictitious Disney World family vacation).

Our approach. Native 2FA from day one, not delegated to whether the firm runs SSO. On top of that, the vault model, which is a stronger claim than anything Rogo makes: per firm, a single-tenant deployment into the bank's own cloud (VPC plus Bedrock) so their data never sits on our infrastructure; per deal, cryptographic isolation with its own KMS key and append-only audit ledger; on close, the deal freezes to immutable 17a-4-compliant cold storage. The sentence for the compliance officer is that their data lives in their own cloud account, every deal is sealed under its own key, and closed deals become FINRA-ready archives producible in a day.

Compliance: reactive versus proactive

What Rogo does. Nothing here, and that is the opening. Rogo is a workflow and answer tool; compliance is left to the firm's existing after-the-fact processes.

Our approach. This is the real differentiator, so it should be built as a first-class surface rather than a footnote. Compliance flips from reactive to proactive: the AI screens everything before it leaves the building, and agents cross-check other agents. The email gate is the concrete version (an Outlook plugin plus Exchange transport rules plus a code-named project inbox, with green, yellow, and red pre-send scoring, where red cannot send until it is rewritten). Every prompt and output logs to the audit trail with a hash of the policy version in force at that moment, so you can always prove which policy governed any historical action. Regulators did not ban frontier models; they banned unsupervised, unlogged, un-walled use of them. That is a product spec, and it is one Rogo has not written.

Spend and usage visibility

What Rogo does. The billing plumbing exists (CostGuard polls cost every five minutes and can hard-block the input bar), but it is denominated in opaque credits, gated behind a beta admin-only Credit Center, and never shown to the individual. There is no way for a user to see how many tokens they are burning or what they are spending, which is a real gap given that the data vendors themselves (FactSet, Moody's) now flag AI token cost as the live question for their own customers.

Our approach. Token and dollar tracking per person and per deal, as a standing surface, not a hidden admin credit meter. Cost attribution is one of the jobs of the rails, so every deliverable already knows which deal and which person drove the spend. A senior user can see burn, set a budget, and price a piece of work before running it.

The moat: compounding deal memory

What Rogo does. Its genuine moat is licensed data (Capital IQ, PitchBook, Preqin, Moody's, Third Bridge, plus VDR access to Ideals and Intralinks) and banker-format output. But that data is licensable by anyone, the analyst-execution layer it automates is the most model-substitutable work in the building, and a real bank found the output weak enough to walk away.

Our approach. The honest moat is not the model or even the data, it is the firm's own compounding deal memory. Every teaser, buyer-list outcome, negotiation pattern, and "we tried this structure and it died in diligence" becomes queryable institutional knowledge that survives the banker who leaves. That solves the bank's oldest problem, which is that know-how walks out the door in people's heads, and it makes churn irrational: switching off in year three means abandoning three years of accumulated deal intelligence. The bank owns the corpus contractually; we own the rails that make it useful. Make the compounding visible (precedent search across their own deals, auto-captured retrospectives, a time-to-first-draft metric that trends down each quarter) because invisible learning does not renew contracts.

What we deliberately do not copy

Rogo spent more than half of its recent updates on the PowerPoint and Excel plugins and on features that mirror Claude and ChatGPT. That is the analyst-deliverable treadmill, and it is both the most commoditizable work and the reason a bank can try the tool and revert to what it had. We should not race Rogo there. The senior seat, the governed rails, the vault, and the compounding memory are the ground Rogo has left open, and they are the ground that is hard to copy.
