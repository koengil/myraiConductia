Conductia — the engineering team's handbook

Conductia is a governed AI workflow that runs a live sell-side M&A deal at an investment bank. This is the team's shared reference for building it: one vocabulary, one mental model of the domain and the architecture, and a place to send every new engineer so the whole team reasons about the system the same way. No finance background assumed — read it cover to cover (~2 hours) or jump to your track.

⏱ ~2 hours cover-to-cover · or pick a track 📚 Built from the blueprint pack + verified research ✓ Regulatory claims checked at the source

The one idea — the whole team shares this even if nothing else

An investment bank running a live deal lives under three permanent constraints. Every strange-looking thing about this product traces back to one of them — keep them in shared view:

CONSTRAINT 1

Secrecy

Leaking deal info can be insider trading — a crime. → code names, information barriers, deal-linked ingestion.

CONSTRAINT 2

Supervision

A qualified human must supervise the firm's activity. An unsupervised tool is a violation. → a human gates every external action; agents are capped + killable.

CONSTRAINT 3

Reconstructability

Years later a regulator can demand the complete record, intact. → everything emits an append-only AuditEvent.

From those three, the one invariant that governs the whole codebase falls out automatically. This is the line the entire team designs against:

AI agentsdraft · summarize · retrieve · propose
→
Humans + deterministic codedecide · gate · approve · release

Anything that decides access, approves, enforces a barrier, or picks the authoritative number is plain, testable code or a human — never an LLM. Not because models are bad, but because a regulator will not accept "the model decided" as a control. When a design puts an agent in charge of a permission, an approval, a barrier, or a source-of-truth conflict, it's wrong — and that's a thing any reviewer on the team can name out loud. Hold this in your head and the architecture explains itself.

01 · THE DOMAIN

What investment banking is — and the one thing we build for

An investment bank is an advisor and intermediary in big financial transactions. It gets hired to run a deal and is paid a fee. There are many kinds of IB work. We build for exactly one:

Our scopeSell-side M&A — advising the owner of a company that is being sold, and running the entire sale: find buyers, run the auction, get to a signed deal. Not buy-side. Not IPOs. Not trading. One workflow: a live mandate. That narrowness is a feature.

Conductia is the system of record + operating engine for that live deal — the thing the team works inside all day. AI runs within the workflow. It is not a chatbot bolted onto a bank.

The cast — who's on a deal, and who can say "send it"

A deal team is a hierarchy, and the hierarchy is the approval model. Seniority = authority to release things to the outside world.

VPRuns the deal day-to-day. First reviewer before work goes to the MD.role: vp

Secrecy is the product constraint

This is the section that makes the rest of the architecture click. The reason AI is hard here — and why this product can exist at all — is one acronym.

  • MNPI — secret info that could move a stock price ("Company X is being acquired"). Trading or tipping on it is insider trading, a federal crime. Section 15(g) of the Exchange Act requires firms to have written procedures to prevent its misuse — so the controls below are legally mandatory, not optional. → forces deal-linked ingestion, the sealed envelope, and Permission/Barrier on every operation.
  • Information barriers ("Chinese walls") — internal walls keeping deal secrets from other teams. A central "Control Room" logs every deal; firms keep a watch list (observe) and a restricted list (block); adding someone is a logged "over-the-wall" step. → Barrier is a deterministic query, never an agent.
  • Code names — deals run as "Project Atlas" so identities never leak. The real identity sits behind an access-controlled key; every lookup is logged. → the code-name service redacts before any agent prompt is built.

How long all this takes — and why it matters to the build

A well-run sell-side process typically runs 6–12 months from engagement to close (often cited as 9–12): prep ~8–12 weeks, buyer outreach + first-round IOIs ~4–6 weeks, management meetings and second-round LOIs with 30–60-day exclusivity, then confirmatory diligence to signing ~8–12 weeks. Complex deals stretch to 18 months+. The takeaway: an agent touching deal artifacts holds MNPI for months — so retention, access logging, and wall controls must persist for the whole lifecycle, not a single session.

How a real firm runs the wall — Control Room, watch vs. restricted, over-the-wall

The barriers in §0 aren't abstract; here's the live machinery the Permission/Barrier model is mirroring:

  • Control Room. A compliance group, separate from the deal teams, logs every MNPI-bearing deal in a control database.
  • Watch list vs. restricted list. The watch list is a confidential surveillance list; the restricted list actively blocks firm and employee activity in named securities. Model both as authoritative, queryable allow/deny services agents must check — never self-adjudicate.
  • Over-the-wall (wall-crossing). Bringing someone onto the deal is a formal, consented, logged, time-bounded step. An agent that moves information between a private-side (deal) context and a public-side context is functionally an uncontrolled wall-crossing — the sealed envelope + barriers are what prevent it.

Statutory root: Section 15(g) of the Exchange Act (originally enacted as §15(f), renumbered by Dodd-Frank 2010) requires written procedures to prevent MNPI misuse; FINRA implemented "minimum standards" via NASD Notice 91-45. Per SEC OCIE's information-barriers report.

02 · THE RULEBOOK

The regulations, in engineer terms

Four bodies of rules shape the build. Here's each one, what it actually says, and the one line of "what it means for the build." ✓ verified = checked against the FINRA/SEC source; reconfirm = well-sourced but re-verify before external use.

FINRA Rule 3110 — Supervision ✓ verified

  • 3110(a) — firms must maintain a system to supervise each associated person, reasonably designed to achieve compliance.
  • 3110(b)(1) — that system must include written supervisory procedures (WSPs).
  • 3110(d) — must include a process to review securities transactions to catch possible insider trading.
What it means for the buildThe product is a supervision system. The always-on compliance ribbon, reviewer/approver on every Task, and the runtime caps on every agent Run (an uncapped agent is an unsupervised actor) all exist to satisfy 3110. WSPs live in Policy.

FINRA Rule 2210 — Communications ✓ verified

  • Sorts outbound comms into correspondence (≤25 retail / 30 days), retail (>25 retail), and institutional (only to institutional investors).
  • 2210(b)(1)(A) — a registered principal must approve each retail communication before use.
  • 2210(d)(1) — content must be fair, balanced, not misleading, with a sound basis to evaluate. ✓ verified
⚠ Precision nuance — don't mis-cite this2210's hard pre-approval applies to retail communications. Most sell-side M&A counterparties are institutional (companies, PE sponsors, advisors), so the bulk of deal comms are institutional communications — which get relief from pre-approval (2210(b)(1)(D)), not a blanket exemption: supervision and recordkeeping (the approving principal, dates of first/last use) still apply. One trap — a comm can't be treated as institutional if you have reason to believe it'll be forwarded to a retail investor (live risk when a teaser reaches an individual principal of a target). The product's "MD approves every send" gate is firm policy + 3110 supervision, a safe default that covers the occasional individual buyer too. So don't tell a banker "2210 requires the MD to approve this email to a PE fund." Good firm policy does; the rule's hard pre-approval is a retail trigger.
What it means for the buildPre-Send QC (the deterministic screen) operationalizes 2210's content + retention discipline — claims-sourced, figures-match, code-name-intact, approval-present — with no LLM in the verdict. Every send is retained as a Communication + AuditEvent.

FINRA Notice 24-09 — AI gets no grace period ✓ verified

The most directly relevant guidance for this team. FINRA Regulatory Notice 24-09 (June 2024) states that all existing FINRA rules and the federal securities laws apply to generative AI and LLMs — technology-neutral, firm-built or third-party. It adds no new requirements, but expressly flags supervision (Rule 3110), recordkeeping, Reg BI, communications (Rule 2210 — AI-generated content is still firm communication), customer-information protection, and model accuracy / "hallucination" risk.

What it means for the buildDeploying an AI agent at a broker-dealer creates no regulatory grace period. Supervisory review, books-and-records capture of AI inputs/outputs (every ModelCall), and 2210 content standards attach from day one. This is why the invariant exists — it isn't gold-plating, it's the rulebook applied to agents.

SEC Rule 17a-4 — Recordkeeping ✓ verified

The broker-dealer recordkeeping/retention rule: records (incl. communications) must be preserved, tamper-evident, retained for years, and promptly producible to regulators. Communications: ≥3 years (17a-4(b)(4)); FINRA Rule 4511 sets a 6-year default otherwise.

⚠ Correction to carry back to the teamThe blueprint says 17a-4 requires WORM (write-once-read-many) storage. That changed. The SEC's Oct 12, 2022 amendment (Release 34-96034) kept WORM as one option and added an "audit-trail alternative": a system that preserves records so an original can be recreated if modified or deleted (time-stamped, capturing who changed what) satisfies 17a-4 without WORM. We comply by meeting EITHER WORM OR the audit-trail requirement.
Why this is good newsConductia's append-only AuditEvent event-sourcing model can itself be the compliance mechanism under the audit-trail option — you may not strictly need certified WORM media (e.g. S3 Object Lock). This shifts the audit store from "buy WORM" toward "build a rigorous audit trail." Still validate the final design with compliance counsel ("permits recreation" is a real bar).

Two-party-consent recording — a hard legal wall

Some U.S. states require everyone on a call to consent before recording. Recording without it is an illegal wiretap. → In V1, capture email + meeting notes only; live call recording is gated on a per-jurisdiction consent flow + legal sign-off (decision Q7). Don't ship "record every call" as just another connector.

03 · ARCHITECTURE & VOCABULARY

The entity model and the one boundary that matters

Everything hangs off one root, Deal. "Mandate / project / transaction" are display aliases — never separate tables. Here's the spine:

Deal ──< Phase ──< Task ──(mode≠human)── Run ──< ModelCall ├──< Communication ──spawns──> Task ──cites──> Artifact ├──< Counterparty (always code-named) └──< AuditEvent (append-only; everything emits it) Permission / Barrier · Approval / Gate · Policy → deterministic backend, NOT agents

The schema, one level down — fields a backend engineer needs

The spine above shows the relationships; here's the shape of each row. Not full DDL — enough to see the columns that carry governance. Two fields do the heavy lifting: the access-controlled realIdentityKeyRef (the code-name perimeter) and the append-only AuditEvent (the 17a-4 substrate).

The two load-bearing fieldsrealIdentityKeyRef is never a plaintext name on the row — it's a reference into a separately access-controlled key store. The real identity is resolved only on an authorized deref, and every deref writes an AuditEvent. That is the code-name perimeter in one column. AuditEvent is the one table where UPDATE and DELETE must be physically impossible (not just discouraged) — make it append-only at the database grant level, because it is the record a regulator reconstructs the deal from years later.
Why there's no Mandate, Job, or stored-Run table
  • No Mandate/Project table. "Mandate" is the engaged state of a Deal; "project"/"transaction" are display synonyms. One root, aliases in the UI.
  • Job is not stored. It's the typed dispatch envelope (next) — the call shape that produces a Run. The Run + its ModelCalls are what persist.
  • Approval is polymorphic. One table; subjectRef points at a Task, Phase, Communication, Artifact, or an access-change — so the gate engine has one code path.
  • Artifact.authority is a rank, not an entity. "Model of record" is the precedence resolver, not a separate "source of truth" table.

The invariant, restated for code

This is the single most important line in the system. Think CQRS: agents are pure producers on the query/propose side; deterministic code + humans own the command/commit side.

CapabilityModeled asNOT
"Can this actor see/send this?"Permission/Barrier queryan agent deciding access
"Does this need sign-off?"Gate rule + Approval eventan agent "approving"
"Which number is right?"precedence function over Artifact.authorityan agent adjudicating
Pre-send checksdeterministic compliance screenan LLM "reviewing"
Runtime caps (steps/tokens/kill)Run governanceagent self-policing
The sealed envelopeAn AgentActor never touches raw firm data. It receives only a permission-filtered, code-name-redacted context envelope, and returns a proposal back through the screen. Barriers/MNPI are enforced before the model sees anything — so swapping models changes nothing about the data boundary. (Think: tokenize the card number before it ever reaches the processor's logs.)

The Job contract — the typed envelope that makes a Run governable

Every non-human Task is dispatched as a Job: a fixed, logged shape declared before the agent runs. It's the single most important data structure in the agent layer — the difference between "an agent we hope behaves" and "an agent that is supervised by construction" (FINRA 3110).

Job { dealId, taskId, phaseId, mode, // agent | both context, // <- the SEALED ENVELOPE: permission-scoped, code-name-redacted allowedTools, forbiddenTools, scopeCredentials, modelPolicy, // sensitivity / cost / latency / quality -> the router budget { // <- declared up front, not discovered after maxSteps, maxTokens, maxCost }, stopConditions, // done | failure | escalation reviewer, approver, riskTier, expectedOutputs, // Artifact / Communication types this Job may produce evidenceRequirements // what must be cited / logged to satisfy the Gate } // executing a Job -> a Run, which emits ModelCalls and AuditEvents
Why declaring budget + tools + stop-conditions up front is the whole gameAn agent with no declared ceiling is, in regulatory terms, an unsupervised actor — and an unsupervised actor is a 3110 violation. By fixing budget, allowedTools, and stopConditions in the envelope before dispatch, the runtime can enforce caps, kill a runaway, and prove afterward exactly what the agent was permitted to do. Supervision becomes a property of the Run's inputs, not a hope about its behavior.

The spine of the whole training: each banking idea, the requirement it forces, and what breaks if we get it wrong. Filter it:

Eight mental models (the analogies that make it stick)
  • Information barriers ≈ row-level security / multi-tenant isolation. A deal is a tenant; MNPI is tenant data; a clean team is a nested tenant. Enforce in the data layer, never the UI.
  • Code names ≈ tokenization / PII redaction at the boundary. Redact before anything reaches a model or a log. Every detokenization is logged.
  • Audit trail ≈ append-only event log / event sourcing — with regulatory teeth. Make UPDATE/DELETE on audit rows impossible, not discouraged.
  • "Agents never decide" ≈ CQRS / pure functions vs. side-effecting commands. When someone's tempted to "let the agent just approve it" — that's the bug.
  • Human gate on irreversible actions ≈ the confirm step before DROP TABLE / a force-push. The dangerous bug is mis-classifying an irreversible task as reversible.
  • Model of record ≈ single source of truth + a deterministic resolver. An unsourced figure blocks the send like a failing referential-integrity check.
  • Pre-Send QC ≈ a deterministic CI gate / pre-commit hook on the firm boundary. The agent cleans the draft; the gate is code; the merge (release) is a human.
  • Runtime control ≈ resource limits / circuit breakers / a sandbox with a kill switch. An uncapped agent is an unsupervised one (3110 again).

Testing strategy — test the gates like a safety system

A corollary of the build-vs-plug-in rule: anything you'd build because a FINRA examiner could request it, you test like a safety system. The deterministic governance core isn't "high coverage would be nice" — it's the part where a silent bug ships a leak or an unapproved send. Test priority follows blast radius, not code volume.

SAFETY-CRITICAL

Pre-Send QC screen

The lib/compliance/screen.ts core. Unit + golden tests; invariant: 100% of covered sends pass QC before release.

SAFETY-CRITICAL

Permission / Barrier

Deny-by-default tests: the absence of a grant must block, and a mis-tag must fail closed, never open.

SAFETY-CRITICAL

Approval / Gate

No release without a logged Approval; irreversible Tasks cannot complete agent-only.

Permission / BarrierUnit, deny-by-defaultNo matching grant ⇒ deny. "Show-exists-but-withhold-contents" honored. A mis-tag fails closed (sev-1 class).
Approval / GateUnit + integrationExternal send / material release is impossible without a logged Approval (default VP→MD).
Sealed-envelope retrievalIntegration (adversarial)Raw firm data / cross-wall MNPI never reaches the model context — an SPOF; test as first-class.
Run runtime controlUnit + integrationBudgets enforced; loop detected; kill switch halts; an agent can never send externally.
End-to-endGolden-pathThe happy deal flow (the existing golden-path seed) stays green as the backend lands.
Start from the seeds you already haveThe repo's tests/ already carries vitest suites for compliance, screening-overlay, and golden-path. Productionizing screen.ts means expanding the compliance golden set until every covered send class has a frozen pass/block case — that suite is your 100%-pass-QC proof, and it's the artifact you show an examiner.

The point of all three is the same one line: agents propose; deterministic code and humans decide, gate, and release.

1 · Deal standup — an MD opens Project Atlas

An MD has just won the sell-side mandate to sell a software company. Before a single document lands, the MNPI perimeter has to exist. This is Issue 1.1 — mode human, agent pre-fills defaults, approver MD, risk sensitive. Nothing here is an LLM judgment: creating a deal is deterministic setup plus a human sign-off.

The origination agent suggests a team roster, a starter task plan, and a buyer hypothesis as editable defaults. It proposes; it cannot create the deal, grant access, or set a barrier. Every value lands in a field the MD can override.

2 · A buyer’s diligence question, end to end

The deal is in marketing / diligence. A buyer — code-named “Counterparty C” — emails a question: “What was FY24 recurring revenue?” This is the day-in-the-life made traceable: Issue 3.1 (capture) → Issue 4.1 (source-grounded answer) → Issue 3.2 (Pre-Send QC). Watch the badge colour move from amber (capture) to indigo (the agent drafts) to amber (the gate) to green (the humans release) — and never the other way.

surface: Comms + Pre-Send QCCommunicationTaskRunModelCallArtifact.authorityGate / Approval
1

Deal-linked ingestion creates a Communication (channel: email, direction: inbound) with code-named participants and a preserved copy. The buyer’s real identity never enters the record un-tokenized.

AuditEvent ✎ verb:communication.captured subject:Comm:atlas-114
2
The Communication spawns a Task Deterministic code · routes

The captured question deterministically spawns a diligence-answer Task — mode: both, reviewer: vp, approver: md, riskTier: irreversible (it will leave the firm). The orchestrator routes it; it does not answer it.

AuditEvent ✎ verb:task.spawned from:Comm:atlas-114
3

The governed retrieval service builds a sealed context envelope: permission-filtered, code-name-redacted, scoped to this deal. The Compliance Comms agent (Run under a budgeted Job) drafts an answer, pulling the figure from the model of record (Artifact.authority) and citing it. Each model request is one logged ModelCall. The agent never saw raw firm data and cannot send.

AuditEvent ✎ verb:run.completed · each ModelCall logs prompt/output/sources/tokens/cost

Before anything can leave the firm the draft runs the Pre-Send QC screen — plain code, no LLM in the verdict: recipient ✓ · attachment ✓ · code-name-intact ✓ · every figure matches Artifact.authority ✓ · claims-sourced ✓ · no hidden metadata ✓ · required Approval present ✓. AuditEvent ✎ verb:approval.recorded reviewer:vp approver:md
6

Only now — gate passed and Approval present — does deterministic code release the email. The outbound is retained as a Communication with its full lineage: the inbound it answers, the Run/ModelCalls that drafted it, the cited Artifact, the screen result, and the approval.

AuditEvent ✎ verb:communication.sent · full chain reconstructable for 17a-4 export
Where the boundary livesSteps 1, 2, 4, 6 are deterministic code. Step 3 is the only agent step — and it is sealed (redacted input) and capped (a budgeted Run) and cannot send. Step 5 is the human gate. The agent cleaned and drafted; the gate is code; the release is a human. Move the figure check out of code and into the model and you’ve broken the compliance unlock.

3 · Pre-Send QC blocks a send, then passes

Same machine, different outcome. An associate is releasing a CIM excerpt to an NDA-signed buyer. The draft contains a headline figure — “42% YoY growth” — that no Artifact.authority source backs. The deterministic screen catches it exactly like a failing referential-integrity check. This is the Issue 3.2 / Issue 2.3 acceptance criterion in motion: an unsourced figure blocks release until resolved.

surface: Comms + Pre-Send QCArtifact (cim excerpt)Artifact.authorityGateApprovalAuditEvent
1
Agent drafts the CIM excerpt Agent · proposes

From the sealed envelope, the agent assembles the excerpt Artifact (type: cim) and the cover note. One figure — “42% YoY growth” — has no provenance link to the model of record; the agent surfaced a number it could not source. That is allowed — drafting is the agent’s job. Catching it is not.

2
Pre-Send QC BLOCKS the send Deterministic code · gates

The screen runs and stops at the figures check: figures vs Artifact.authority fails — “42%” resolves to no authoritative source. Verdict is BLOCK, deterministic and reproducible, with the reason attached. No LLM was consulted and no human override is offered at this layer; the send simply cannot proceed.

⛔ BLOCKED — unsourced figure. "42% YoY growth" has no Artifact.authority match. Release halted, like a failing referential-integrity constraint.
AuditEvent ✎ verb:screen.blocked reason:figure_unsourced value:"42%"
3
A human sources the figure to Artifact.authority Human · decides

The associate opens the model of record, confirms the correct number, and binds the claim to its authoritative source (or corrects it). The fix is a human action against the source of truth — not the agent “trying again,” and not the screen being relaxed.

AuditEvent ✎ verb:figure.sourced to:Artifact:model-of-record#rev_growth
4
Re-run — the screen now PASSES Deterministic code · gates

The identical deterministic screen runs again. Every figure now maps to an Artifact.authority source; recipient, code name, claims, and metadata all check. Verdict PASS — same code, same inputs minus the one defect, reproducible result.

AuditEvent ✎ verb:screen.passed subject:Artifact:cim-excerpt-7 (v2)
5
MD Approval, then release Human · approves

With a clean screen, the MD records the Approval and deterministic code releases the excerpt, retained as an Artifact + Communication. The full block→source→pass→approve history is preserved — a regulator can see the firm caught its own error and the gate held.

AuditEvent ✎ verb:approval.recorded · verb:communication.sent · block + fix retained in the chain
⚠ The bug to never writeThe fix in step 3 is a human sourcing the figure and a re-run of the deterministic screen — never an LLM “deciding” the figure is close enough, and never a path that lets the agent or a user wave the block through. The moment a model gets a vote in that verdict, “the model decided” becomes the firm’s defense to a regulator, and there is no defense in that sentence.
✓
Across all three traces, the same shape holds
  • Agents only ever produce a draft from a sealed envelope — capped, redacted, unable to send.
  • Deterministic code creates access, captures, and runs the gate — plain, testable, no LLM in any verdict (every amber step).
  • Humans are the only actor that decides and releases — recorded as an Approval (every green step).
  • An AuditEvent is written at every state change, so the whole chain reconstructs years later.

05 · BUILD VS. PLUG-IN

What's our moat, what do we integrate

The one ruleIf a component would appear in a FINRA examiner's record request — build it, deterministically, test it like a safety system. If it'd appear on an AWS/SaaS invoice — buy it. Agents live in between: they draft, bought models power them, the built core always gets the final gated word.
ComponentVerdictWhy
Pre-Send QC / compliance screenBUILD ⭐The crown jewel — the compliance unlock; a seed already exists
Permissions + barriers + code-name serviceBUILDThe MNPI perimeter; banking barrier semantics no vendor sells
Approval/Gate engine + precedence resolverBUILDThe §12 human gates + §11.1 conflict logic — proprietary
Orchestrator / conductorBUILDOnly the governance-coupling (task→risk→gate→approval), not generic scheduling
Audit / recordkeeping storeHYBRID→BUILDYour audit trail can now be the 17a-4 mechanism (no WORM required)
Ingestion + connectorsHYBRIDBuy the connectors; build the tag/deal-link/permission layer
Model router + cost instrumentationHYBRIDBuild sensitivity-routing + ModelCall audit; buy the gateway
Search / retrieval (RAG)HYBRIDBuy the vector store; build the permission-aware filter + provenance
Runtime control (sandbox/kill)HYBRIDBuild the budget/kill policy; buy the sandbox isolation
Foundation models (LLMs)PLUG-INStateless, swappable; off-thesis to build
Datastore / persistencePLUG-INPostgres/Supabase; the schema/contract over it is yours
The four UI surfacesBUILD (thin)Proprietary assembly of the engine; keep the shell deliberately light

The moat is the deterministic governance core — everything that decides, gates, or releases. The commodity is everything that drafts, moves bytes, or runs inference. Self-built sandbox/WORM are liabilities, not assets.

The risks the pack under-states

HIGH

Greenfield backend

~0% of the governed spine exists. Timeline is "build a backend," not "wire up a prototype."

HIGH

Model-of-record hole

Pre-Send QC must verify figures, but V1 builds no model + bans LLM math. Resolve Q8 + Q10 together.

HIGH

Legal go/no-gos

MNPI over-collection, two-party consent, 17a-4 retention — closer to gates than scope dials.

HIGH

Un-measurable pricing

Net Labor Value needs in-tenant effort telemetry that may be privacy-blocked or API-impossible.

HIGH

Two load-bearing SPOFs

Orchestrator + sealed-envelope retrieval are greenfield and under-specified. Harden + test as first-class.

MED

Deterministic data

"Permissions aren't AI" only helps if the tagging pipeline is right. A mis-tag passes a leak.

The honest one-linerThe thinking is well ahead of the code. The strategy is sound and the moat is correctly located — but the governed backend is essentially greenfield, and three decisions + a non-negotiable "sprint 0" stand between this plan and a credible pilot.
