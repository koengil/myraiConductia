# Narrative Arc and Positioning

This skill teaches how the best recent IPO roadshow decks tell their story: the arc they follow, the positioning moves they make, how they open, how they close. It is built from twelve decks spanning infrastructure (CoreWeave), design (Figma), fintech (Chime), vertical SaaS (ServiceTitan), horizontal SaaS (Klaviyo, Navan, OneStream), security (Netskope, Rubrik, SailPoint), and digital health (Hinge Health, Omada).

**Scope: purely narrative and positioning.** Financials, unit economics, valuation comps, and design are out of scope.

## How to use this skill

1. **Start with the canonical arc.** Almost every deck follows the 8-stage structure below. Use it as scaffolding, not as a straitjacket.
2. **Pick your positioning moves.** From the repertoire, select 3–5 that fit the company. Don't try to run all of them.
3. **Design the opening hook and closing move first.** They do the most load-bearing work. If they don't land, the middle 30 slides can't save you.
4. **Write every slide title as an action title with positive charge.** Every title must make a claim, not label a topic — and every title must read as a boast, an opportunity, or an outcome to want, not a flat fact. See *Slide titles: every title is an argument* below. This rule applies to every title you suggest, in every stage, opening, and close.
5. **Sequence titles within each section.** After writing the action titles for a section, read them as a sequence — without the slide bodies — and rewrite for escalation, connective tissue, and a landing title. See *Title sequencing: each section's titles tell a sub-story*. The default is two passes: per-slide first, per-section second.
6. **Read the reference files for examples.** Each deck's analysis is in `references/<slug>.md`. When the user's situation resembles one of them (e.g. re-IPO → SailPoint, category creation in infrastructure → CoreWeave, vertical SaaS with authenticity → ServiceTitan), load that reference for deep-dive patterns.

## Slide titles: every title is an argument

**Rule:** Every suggested slide title must be an *action title* — a complete claim the slide proves — not a topic label. If a title could sit unchanged on a competitor's slide, it is a label, not a title.

**Test:** Read the title aloud, then ask "so what?" If the title already answers the "so what," it's an action title. If it doesn't, rewrite it.

**Tone rule: every title carries positive charge.** A title isn't just a claim — it's a claim worth defending out loud. Read it aloud. If an investor would hear it as a *boast* (pride, scale), an *opportunity* (urgency, inevitability), or an *outcome to want* (aspiration), it lands. If it sounds like a 10-K bullet or a Wikipedia entry, it's flat — rewrite it.

**Flat → charged rewrites:**
- "We onboard customers in two weeks" → "We onboard 10x faster than the legacy alternative"
- "We have 6.9 billion profiles" → "6.9 billion profiles compound into a moat new entrants can't catch"
- "Our platform consolidates eight tools" → "One platform retires eight legacy point tools"
- "Net retention is 132%" → "We grow inside every account we land — 132% net retention proves it"

**Tone anti-patterns:** hedging language ("could," "may," "we believe"), generic enthusiasm ("amazing," "powerful," "best-in-class," "world-class"), passive voice, marketing fluff that doesn't carry a number or a verb. Charge comes from *specificity*, not from adjectives.

**Anti-patterns (do not suggest titles like these):**
- "Industry Overview" → use "The legacy stack is breaking under AI workloads"
- "Our Solution" → use "One platform replaces eight point tools"
- "Market Opportunity" → use "$120B market, only 4% penetrated"
- "Customers" → use "8 of the top 10 banks run on us"
- "Why Now" → use "Three forces are converging in our favor"
- "Financial Highlights" → use "We compound at Rule of 60 with expanding margins"
- "Product Architecture" → use "A unified data layer makes every module sharper than the last"
- "Competitive Landscape" → use "Legacy vendors can't ship cloud-native; cloud-native vendors can't ship enterprise"

**Construction patterns that produce strong action titles:**
- **Subject + verb + object.** "AI is rewriting the data stack."
- **Quantified claim.** "95% of consumers fail at spending discipline."
- **Before/after.** "From backup utility to cyber resilience platform."
- **Comparison.** "10x faster onboarding than the legacy alternative."
- **Inevitability.** "The next billion sessions will be agent-driven."
- **Identity claim.** "We are the operating system for the trades."

**Length guide:** 6–14 words. Long enough to carry a verb and an object. Short enough to read in one breath.

When you suggest titles for any slide in any stage below, follow this rule without exception.

## Title sequencing: each section's titles tell a sub-story

The canonical 8-stage arc handles the deck-level story. This rule handles the *sub-section* story: the 3–6 titles inside a single stage (e.g., the four slides that make up your "moat" section, or the three slides that make up "why now").

**Rule:** Read the titles of any section in order, with the slide bodies hidden. The sequence alone should make the section's argument escalate, connect, and land. If the four titles could be shuffled without losing meaning, the sequence has failed.

**Three sub-arc shapes that work:**

- **Stack-and-land.** Each title raises the stakes; the last one resolves. *Example (why-now):* "Legacy EPM is reaching end-of-life." → "Finance teams need AI-grade tooling now." → "And the buying window closes inside 18 months." The first two set up; the third lands.
- **Frame → prove → moat.** Three-slide product section. *Example:* "One platform retires eight legacy point tools." → "Eight of the top ten banks already run it." → "And every new module sharpens the data layer underneath."
- **Bookend.** Open and close the section on the same metaphor or claim. *Example (moat):* opening title "Born in the trades, built for the trades." → middle proof titles → closing title "Twenty years in the trades is a moat new entrants can't buy."

**Process: write titles in two passes.**

1. **Pass 1 (per slide).** Write each slide's action title against the slide content. Apply the action-title rule and the tone rule.
2. **Pass 2 (per section).** Read all of the section's titles in order, *without the slide bodies.* Ask: does the sequence escalate? Is there connective tissue (a recurring metaphor, a callback, a number that grows)? Does the final title *land* — i.e., does it pay off the setup? Rewrite for arc, not for individual punch. Drop or merge any title that doesn't move the section's argument forward.

The second pass is what separates a section that *reads* like a deck from a section that *lands* like one. Do not skip it.

**Sequence anti-patterns:**
- Six independently strong titles that don't share a thread.
- A "build" sequence with no climax — three setups and no payoff.
- Repeating the same claim three different ways instead of escalating.
- A bookend that drops the metaphor in the middle slides.

## The canonical IPO narrative arc (8 stages)

Most decks run 20–60 slides. The stages below compress into fewer slides in shorter decks (CoreWeave's 20) and expand in longer ones (Rubrik's 62, ServiceTitan's 61). The **sequence** is what's consistent, not the page count.

### Stage 1 — Cover + offering summary (slides 1–3)
Tagline on cover. Safe harbor / offering summary. Usually skippable, but the cover tagline is critical — it's the first positioning claim (see *Signature cover taglines* below).

*Action-title examples for the cover/tagline:* "Powering AI Innovation" (CoreWeave), "Defining the next generation of identity security" (SailPoint), "Securing the world's data" (Rubrik). Each is a verb-led claim. Avoid "Company Overview," "Investor Presentation," or any title that just names the meeting.

### Stage 2 — Mission / vision / one-liner (slide 2–4)
The company in a single sentence. Often an aspirational phrase tied to a population ("everyday Americans"), an outcome ("turn ideas into the world's best digital products"), or a category claim ("The operating system for modern finance"). This is what the investor should be able to repeat back.

*Action-title examples:* "We unite everyday people to unlock financial progress." "We are the operating system for modern finance." "We turn ideas into the world's best digital products." Avoid "Our Mission," "Vision," "Who We Are."

### Stage 3 — "Why now" / problem (slides 4–8)
Macro drivers that make the problem urgent: AI explosion, cloud adoption, ransomware era, data proliferation, finance talent shortage, regulatory shifts, underserved populations. The best decks quantify the problem (Chime: "95% fail at spending discipline"; Hinge: "MSK = ~40% of medical spend, largely ignored"). Weak decks leave this as vibes.

*Action-title examples:* "95% of Americans fail at spending discipline." "MSK pain consumes 40% of medical spend — and goes largely untreated." "Legacy EPM is reaching end-of-life just as finance teams need AI." Avoid "Market Problem," "Industry Challenges," "Why Now" as a bare label.

### Stage 4 — Solution architecture (slides 8–15)
The platform as a diagram, usually concentric circles or layered stacks. Core + extensions. Often labelled with a category claim ("Rubrik Security Cloud", "OneStream operating system", "Netskope One Gateway"). Include 1–2 marquee capabilities that are hardest to replicate.

*Action-title examples:* "One platform replaces eight point tools." "A unified data layer makes every module sharper than the last." "Rubrik Security Cloud delivers cyber resilience end-to-end." Avoid "Our Platform," "Solution Overview," "Product Architecture."

### Stage 5 — Proof (slides 12–20)
Customer logos at scale. Outcome metrics (pain reduction, cost savings, engagement). Deployment wins across verticals or segments. Marquee names (Fortune 500 counts, OpenAI deal, payer penetration). This is where belief compounds.

*Action-title examples:* "8 of the top 10 banks run on us." "Members cut pain by 68% in 12 weeks." "OpenAI runs production workloads on CoreWeave." Avoid "Customers," "Case Studies," "Proof Points."

### Stage 6 — Moat (slides 18–25)
Why competitors can't easily replicate: data scale (Klaviyo's 6.9B profiles), network / habit lock (Chime's 54x/month check-ins), ecosystem + partners (OneStream's 90+ apps), category-ownership (CoreWeave's "AI Hyperscaler"), founder authenticity (ServiceTitan "Born in the Trades"). Name the moat explicitly.

*Action-title examples:* "6.9 billion profiles compound into a moat new entrants can't catch." "Members check Chime 54 times a month — habit, not feature." "Born in the trades, built for the trades." Avoid "Competitive Advantage," "Why Us," "Differentiation."

### Stage 7 — Business model, growth levers, financial trajectory (slides 20–40)
ARR, growth, NRR, gross margin, path to profitability. Frame as durability, not hype. Name 4–6 explicit growth levers for the post-IPO period (Figma's 6 levers, Chime's 6 vectors, OneStream's 4 pillars). The levers *are* the ask.

*Action-title examples:* "We compound at Rule of 60 with expanding margins." "132% net retention proves we grow inside every account we land." "Six levers extend the runway for the next decade." Avoid "Financial Highlights," "Growth Strategy," "Business Model."

### Stage 8 — Closing move (final 2–5 slides)
Either (a) "Now is the time" inflection (ServiceTitan), (b) strategic-position convergence diagram (Rubrik: AI × Data × Security), (c) vision restatement that bookends the opener (OneStream), or (d) key-takeaways enumeration (Netskope, SailPoint). Followed by appendix — reconciliations and definitions that signal institutional rigor.

*Action-title examples:* "Now is the time to modernize the trades." "AI, data, and security converge on a single control plane." "We are still just getting started." Avoid "Conclusion," "Summary," "Key Takeaways" as a bare label — number the takeaways and load each one with a claim.

## Positioning moves (the repertoire)

Pick 3–5 that fit. Named moves with an example of how each deck executes.

### Category moves

- **Category creation.** Name a new category, claim to own it. CoreWeave: "The AI Hyperscaler." Chime's "SpendBank" labels a checking account as a new category. Use when the incumbent category frames you as a derivative.

- **Category redefinition.** Take an existing category and reframe what it is. Rubrik: from "backup" to "cyber resilience." Figma: from "design tool" to "product development platform." Use when the existing category undersells you.

- **"Operating system for X."** Claim the role of the root system rather than an app. OneStream: "the operating system for modern finance." ServiceTitan: "the Operating System for the Trades." Implies ubiquity + indispensability. Most powerful in verticals or functions with fragmented incumbents.

- **Analogical positioning ("X for Y").** Navan: "The Amazon for Travel." Figma: "system of record for design development." Borrows belief from an established mental model. Cheap if overused, powerful if earned.

### Contrast moves

- **Incumbent as villain.** Chime: "Traditional banks structurally cannot serve everyday Americans." Strongest when backed by behavioral data, not rhetoric.

- **Implicit incumbent contrast.** Figma doesn't name competitors; it contrasts historical tool fragmentation against its unified platform. Preferred when you already lead and naming rivals would legitimize them.

- **Legacy end-of-life framing.** OneStream: legacy EPM (Oracle Hyperion, SAP) is "reaching end-of-life." Netskope: "legacy infrastructure can't address the modern digital landscape." Pairs well with "Why now" — the legacy wave is cresting.

- **Before/after via roadmap.** Figma's product-launch timeline (FigJam → Dev Mode → Slides → Draw → Sites → Buzz) proves continuous expansion without calling out competitors.

### Authenticity moves

- **Mission as moat.** Chime leads with "unite everyday people." ServiceTitan: "Born in the Trades, Built for the Trades." Works when the mission is structurally inseparable from the business model (Chime's cost advantage *requires* serving everyday Americans; ServiceTitan's product *requires* industry fluency).

- **Founder / origin DNA.** Use founder photos on early slides, quote origin stories, reference "X years in industry Y." Best when authenticity is the differentiator (vertical SaaS, regulated industries).

- **Permission-granting re-introduction.** SailPoint's "(Re)introducing" two-column "what's same / what's different" matrix handles the re-IPO or transformation story without defensiveness. Template for any company going public a second time or after a major pivot.

### Technical / strategic moves

- **AI-native (not bolted on).** Every 2024–2025 deck makes this claim. The differentiator is *proof*: Navan "AI-first since founding"; OneStream's named AI modules (StandardAI, Scenario Clarity, Curate, RaaS) with transparency/auditability principles; Hinge's AI-augmented care teams with human-in-loop. Vague AI claims are free; earned AI claims require named systems + outcomes.

- **Platform consolidation.** Unified replacement for N point solutions. Netskope consolidates SWG + firewall + DLP + SSL inspection. Rubrik consolidates data resilience + observability + remediation. OneStream consolidates 30+ finance systems. Include the count when possible — the larger the consolidation, the stronger the claim.

- **Data / network moat.** Klaviyo: 6.9B unified profiles. Hinge: 5.4M clinical data points. Chime: habit-lock metrics (54x/month). The move is to frame accumulated data or behavior as compounding over time in a way new entrants cannot catch up to.

- **Layered TAM.** Expand TAM via adjacencies, not via bigger market numbers. Figma layers designer → PM → developer → marketer as new seats per persona. Chime layers savings, installment loans, digital cards, workplace as self-contained TAMs. Each layer is credible because it's a known expansion path.

- **Land-and-expand proof.** Customer cohort expansion curves (Figma's 132% NRR, OneStream customer cases at 3–4.4x expansion, ServiceTitan 110% NDR). Converts growth narrative from "new logos" to "existing account depth."

- **"Why now" — macro tailwind anchor.** Every deck explicitly names 2–5 macro forces the company rides. The best "why now" slides are falsifiable (specific drivers, specific dates) rather than general vibes.

### Closing moves

- **"Just getting started" inflection.** Chime and ServiceTitan both close on decades-of-runway framing. Works when the business is already at scale — implies optionality, not hype.

- **Strategic-position convergence diagram.** Rubrik's final slide: AI × Data × Security intersecting. OneStream's vision-statement bookend (opens and closes on "operating system for modern finance"). Gives the investor one image to carry out of the room.

- **Growth-lever enumeration.** Figma's six icons. OneStream's four pillars. The enumeration *is* the ask — each lever is what the IPO capital will unlock.

- **Key-takeaways list.** Netskope and SailPoint end on 6–7 numbered takeaways. Lower ceiling than a convergence diagram, but higher floor — safe close when the narrative has been complex.

## Opening hook patterns (the first 3 slides do the most work)

Every deck's opening falls into one or more of these. Each pattern below ends with a model action title — use that style when suggesting your own.

- **Tagline as cover.** "Powering AI Innovation" (CoreWeave), "Security and Networking ReAimagined" (Netskope), "Defining the next generation of identity security" (SailPoint). One short line containing the category claim. *Action-title style:* verb-led, category-claiming.
- **Mission statement (pp. 2–3).** "To unite everyday people to unlock their financial progress" (Chime). "Securing the World's Data" (Rubrik). Human outcome + scope. *Action-title style:* "We [verb] [population] to [outcome]."
- **"At a glance" scale proof (pp. 3–4).** ARR, customer count, growth rate, dominance metric. Navan fronts $61.3M ARR + 10K+ customers + $87.6B spend managed + 4.1 days saved. Anchors the investor's scale expectation before claims. *Action-title style:* "$61M ARR, 10K customers, $87B managed — at a glance" beats "Company Snapshot."
- **Visual product proof.** Figma's page 4 displays Figma-built UIs (Google, Netflix, Uber) wordlessly. Proves product-market fit before making any claim. *Action-title style:* "Google, Netflix, and Uber design on Figma" beats "Customer Examples."
- **Permission-granting reintroduction.** SailPoint's "same / different" matrix. Use for re-IPOs, turnarounds, strategic resets. *Action-title style:* "(Re)introducing SailPoint: same mission, sharper machine."
- **Named problem with a number.** Chime: 95% fail at spending discipline. Hinge: MSK = 40% of medical spend. Omada: 80%+ members miss engagement opportunities between visits. A quantified problem converts skepticism into curiosity. *Action-title style:* lead with the number and the verb.

Rule: **the cover tagline + first three slides should make the investor able to repeat the one-liner, the category claim, and one scale metric.** If they can't, the hook failed. Every one of those slides must have an action title.

## Closing move patterns (the final 2–5 slides)

Each close needs an action title — never "Conclusion" or "Closing Remarks."

- **Vision bookend.** Open and close on the same sentence. OneStream does this with "the operating system for modern finance." Reinforces a single load-bearing metaphor. *Action-title style:* "We are the operating system for modern finance."
- **Convergence diagram.** Rubrik's AI × Data × Security intersection. One image to remember. *Action-title style:* "AI, data, and security converge on one control plane."
- **"Now is the time."** ServiceTitan's 5-message layer: massive opportunity / decades-long runway / industry standard / changing lives / passionate team. Paired with scale. *Action-title style:* "Now is the time to modernize the trades."
- **Growth-lever icons.** Figma's six-priority closing. The post-IPO roadmap as visual grid. *Action-title style:* "Six levers extend our runway through the next decade."
- **Key takeaways.** Netskope and SailPoint's numbered takeaway list. Each takeaway must itself be an action title — number them and load each line with a verb and an object. *Action-title style:* "1. We own the only cloud-native security fabric at scale." "2. Our platform consolidates eight legacy categories." (Not: "1. Platform.")
- **Appendix as institutional rigor.** Reconciliations, definitions, pro forma cap tables — every deck ends here. Signals seriousness without needing emotional uplift. Appendix titles can be neutral labels; this is the only place that exception applies.

## Common failure modes

- **Topic-label titles.** "Industry Overview," "Our Solution," "Why Now," "Customers" — these are placeholders, not titles. Every body slide must claim something. If the title would survive on a competitor's deck, it has failed.
- **Flat action titles.** A title can be technically action-titled and still emotionally flat. "We onboard customers in two weeks" is action-titled but reads like a 10-K. "We onboard 10x faster than the legacy alternative" lands. If there's no comparison, no quantification, and no inevitability in the title, it's flat — rewrite for charge.
- **Section titles that don't sequence.** Six punchy independent titles that read fine on their own but make no argument when read in order. Test by reading titles only, slide bodies hidden — if the sequence is shuffleable, it isn't a story. Rewrite for escalation and connective tissue.
- **Generic "AI-powered" without proof.** Vague AI mentions read as table stakes. Back it with named systems, outcomes, or architectural principles.
- **TAM inflation.** Claiming a $500B market for a product that addresses 2% of it triggers skepticism. Prefer layered TAMs where each layer is defensible.
- **Feature lists instead of positioning.** A slide listing 12 product capabilities is a data dump, not a narrative beat. Collapse features into moves.
- **Unquantified problem slides.** "Customers face friction" is not a problem slide. "95% fail at spending discipline" is.
- **Origin story without structural payoff.** Founder stories are only moats when the business model *requires* that background (ServiceTitan's trades DNA).
- **Hedged closes.** Ending on non-GAAP reconciliations without a prior emotional close leaves the deck feeling like a 10-K. Lead the close with the thesis; use the appendix for rigor.

## Final rule

Every positioning move in this skill has been used successfully in at least one recent priced IPO. The skill is not to invent novelty — it's to **make deliberate choices** about which proven moves fit the company. Most narratives fail not from using the wrong move, but from using five moves half-heartedly when two done well would have closed the room.
